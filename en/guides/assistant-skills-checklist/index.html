Assistant skills checklist a self-assessment by block, with how to practise each one

Most assistants are good at the parts of the job they have done a lot and quietly weak at the parts they have never been asked to do. A checklist fixes that: you see the whole profession at once, mark honestly where you stand and know what to practise before the next interview or the next review with your principal. This article from our School of assistants series covers six blocks: calendar and travel, inbox and messaging, documents and payments routine, confidentiality, communication, and AI tools. For each line ask yourself: can I do this today without help, and can I prove it with an example. Employers can use the same list to shape a practical skills assessment.

Block 1: calendar and travel

The visible core of the job. Employers judge it first because mistakes here are immediate: a missed flight, a double-booked meeting, a principal in the wrong place.

  • I keep one calendar as the single source of truth, with buffers between meetings and travel time included
  • I resolve a conflict between two senior requests without asking the principal first, then inform them in one line
  • I plan a trip end to end: flights, hotel, ground transport, documents and visa requirements for the trip, a backup option, and a one-page itinerary with times in local time zones
  • I keep a recurring list of renewals and deadlines (passports, Emirates ID, licences, insurance, memberships) and act 60 days ahead
  • I know the principal's preferences by heart: seat, hotel chain, meeting length, when they do not want to be disturbed
  • How to practise: take a real week from your own life, create three conflicts and solve them on paper; build an itinerary template and fill it for a two-city trip with a connection under two hours

Block 2: inbox and messaging

Email, WhatsApp and Telegram are where the principal's time disappears. A good assistant filters, drafts and follows up so the principal reads only what needs them.

  • I triage an inbox in three groups within 20 minutes: needs the principal today, I can handle, can wait or archive
  • I draft replies in the principal's voice that they can send with at most one small edit
  • I keep a follow-up list and chase people who did not answer, politely, on a schedule
  • I separate business, personal and household channels and know what may be forwarded to whom
  • I write short: the first sentence says what is needed and by when
  • How to practise: take 15 real or sample emails, rank them, write three replies of under 60 words each, time yourself; ask a friend to judge whether the replies sound like the principal, not like you

Block 3: documents and payments routine

Not accounting, but the routine around money and paperwork: invoices, receipts, contracts, renewals, simple reports. This block separates a junior from a senior assistant.

  • I keep a filing structure anyone could navigate: by year, by counterparty, by type, with clear names
  • I check an invoice against the agreement before passing it on: amount, dates, bank details, VAT line if present
  • I prepare a payment list for the principal's approval and never move money on my own unless a written rule allows it
  • I track contracts and their renewal or notice dates and flag them in advance
  • I produce a one-page weekly or monthly summary: what was paid, what is pending, what needs a decision
  • How to practise: build a spreadsheet with ten sample invoices and a contract list with renewal dates; set up reminders; write a one-page summary from it in 15 minutes

Block 4: confidentiality

The block that decides trust. It cannot be learned from a course, but it can be made into habits and shown in references.

  • I know which information is private by default: health, family, money, deals, HR matters, addresses and travel plans
  • I decline politely when someone asks for the principal's schedule or data, offer to pass the request on, and tell the principal
  • I use the employer's accounts and devices for work, with strong passwords and two-factor authentication, and I do not keep copies on personal devices
  • I do not discuss the principal's affairs with colleagues, friends or former employers, even in general terms
  • I keep a handover file so that nothing depends on my memory alone, and I understand that it belongs to the employer
  • How to practise: write down your own rules in one page, as if for a successor; ask a former principal whether they would confirm your discretion in a reference call

Block 5: communication and judgement

Tone, timing and the courage to say no on someone else's behalf. This is what separates an assistant from a messenger.

  • I say no politely with an alternative: not this week, but Tuesday at 10 works
  • I give the principal a short, structured update: what happened, what I did, what I need from them
  • I push back when a request is unclear or risky, and I know how to do it respectfully
  • I adapt tone by channel and person: a bank, a minister's office, a child's school and a friend of the family need different registers
  • I switch languages mid-conversation if the role needs it and keep the same quality of writing in each
  • How to practise: write three refusals to different people about the same request; record a 60-second voice update to the principal about a problem and listen to whether it is structured

Block 6: AI tools and digital routine

AI assistants and automation are now part of the job. Used well they save hours; used carelessly they leak data or send a wrong draft. Employers increasingly ask about this block.

  • I use an AI assistant for first drafts, summaries, translations and research, and I check every fact and name before anything leaves my hands
  • I never paste confidential data (names, amounts, health, deals) into a public AI tool unless the employer has approved the tool and its data terms
  • I automate the routine: calendar rules, email filters, templates, recurring reminders, a shared task board
  • I know the main tools of the Dubai office: Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, Zoom and Teams, WhatsApp Business, a CRM or task tool, travel platforms, e-signature
  • I keep a list of the principal's recurring tasks that could be automated or delegated and propose one improvement a month
  • How to practise: take one routine task you do every week and build a template or automation for it; write a one-paragraph policy for yourself on what you will and will not put into AI tools

FAQ

What skills does an assistant need in Dubai?

Six blocks: calendar and travel, inbox and messaging, documents and payments routine, confidentiality, communication and judgement, and AI and digital tools. Business-level English is the base for all of them; Arabic or Russian is often requested on top. Employers test the first two blocks and confidentiality most often in a practical assessment.

How can I improve my skills as an assistant without a course?

Practise on real material: triage your own inbox daily in three groups, build itinerary and summary templates, run a renewals list with 60-day reminders, write refusals in different registers and automate one routine task a month. Keep examples of each so you can show them in an interview.
