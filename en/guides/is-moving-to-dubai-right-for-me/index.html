Is moving to Dubai right for me? a decision framework: motives, gains, costs, runway, offer first, plan B and a self-check

Moving to Dubai for work is one of the few decisions that changes almost everything at once: income, climate, language, friends, distance from family, the pace of your days. People who are happy here two years later usually did not decide on a feeling; they decided on a few honest answers and then let the adventure happen. This guide is for a beauty master, nurse or doctor, assistant, administrator or sales specialist who wants to move but has not decided yet. It walks through why you want it, what you gain and what you give up, how much money to have before the flight, why a signed offer comes before the ticket, what a plan B looks like and ends with ten questions to answer in writing. The mechanics of the move are in our guide on how to move to Dubai for work; this page is about the decision itself.

Talk to us

Start with why: the motive decides how the move feels

The same city is a gift to one person and a trap to another, and the difference is rarely the city. Write your reason down in one sentence and check which of the common motives it is closest to.

  • Income and savings: Dubai pays salaries without personal income tax, so the number in the offer is close to the number you keep; this motive works when you also know what you will spend, see the runway section below
  • Career: a bigger market, international clients, a line in the CV that opens doors elsewhere; works when the role is a real step and not just the same job in a hotter place
  • Safety and order for the family: a motive that works when the whole family wants it, and fails when one person is dragged along
  • Escape from a situation at home: understandable, but a move does not solve a conflict, a burnout or a relationship; name the real problem first, then decide whether Dubai is the answer or just a distance
  • Curiosity and a new chapter: a good motive if the practical side is covered; a bad one if it is the only thing holding the plan
  • Test: if the motive disappeared tomorrow (a raise at home, the conflict resolved), would you still go? If yes, the motive is strong enough

The runway: how much money to have before the flight

Arriving without money for the first months is the most common mistake we see in candidates. The employer pays for the visa and medical insurance, but your own set-up costs come before the first salary, and rent in Dubai is often paid in advance by cheques plus a deposit and an agency fee. Build your own number with the method below instead of trusting a figure from a forum.

  • List the one-off costs: flight, temporary stay for the first two to four weeks, deposit and first rent payment, SIM, basic furniture or a furnished room, a Nol card and taxi rides, document translations and attestations you still need
  • List the monthly costs you will have anyway: rent, utilities (DEWA) and internet, groceries, transport, phone, remittances home, personal care, a small buffer
  • Check each line yourself: rent on Bayut, Dubizzle and Property Finder for the area and type you really would take; groceries at Carrefour or Lulu online; transport at RTA fares; a SIM on the du or e& sites
  • Orientation for a single person (orientation, autumn 2026, check current prices): a room in a shared flat in a mid-range area from about 2,500 to 4,500 AED per month, a studio from about 45,000 to 75,000 AED per year, groceries from about 1,200 to 2,000 AED per month; the spread is wide, which is why you check your own lines
  • Runway rule: have the one-off costs plus two to three months of your monthly costs available before you fly, even with a signed offer; salaries are usually paid monthly after the first month, and some employers help with the first accommodation, many do not
  • Ask the employer in writing what they cover: temporary accommodation, a flight, a joining bonus or salary advance; any of these shortens the runway you need

A signed offer before the ticket, and a plan B

Responsibility in this move has a simple shape: do not fly on promises. The employer who hires you arranges and pays for the work permit and visa; the offer and the contract are what turn an idea into a plan. A plan B is not pessimism; it is what lets you relax once you land.

  • Fly for a job only with a written offer that names the role, the total package (basic salary and allowances), the start date, the probation and notice terms, insurance and who pays which relocation cost; read our guides on the employment contract and on probation and notice
  • Check the employer the way they check you: a licensed salon, clinic or company, a real address, reviews, a video call with the person you will report to; a serious employer is never offended by this
  • Never pay anyone for a job, a visa or a place in a queue; the employer pays for the work permit and visa, and candidates do not pay consultancies either
  • If you come to look first, come as a visitor or on a job seeker visa and plan the trip as a trip, with a return date and money for it; see our job seeker visa guide and check the current rules on the official portals (ICP, GDRFA)
  • Plan B, written down: how long you give the first job, what you do if it does not work out, where the money for a return flight sits, which contacts at home you keep warm, and what you will have gained even in the worst case (English, references, a Dubai line in the CV)
  • Tell the family the plan, the runway and the plan B; a move agreed on numbers survives the hard weeks better than a move agreed on hope

The 10-question self-check

Answer in writing, honestly, with dates and numbers. Seven or more clear yes answers usually mean you are ready to look for an offer seriously; fewer means there is specific work to do first, not that the move is wrong.

  • 1. Can I say in one sentence why I want to move, and would I still go if that reason disappeared?
  • 2. Do I know the realistic salary range for my role in Dubai, from our Salary Index or similar sources, and does it cover the monthly costs I have checked myself?
  • 3. Do I have the one-off costs plus two to three months of living costs available, separate from the money I keep for the family at home?
  • 4. Is my English enough to work and handle a landlord, a bank and a clinic, and if not, have I started fixing it?
  • 5. For medics: do I know my licensing path (DataFlow, DHA steps) and how long it may realistically take, in months?
  • 6. Is my CV in the Dubai format with visa status, availability date, languages and portfolio, and do I have two references ready?
  • 7. Have I spoken to at least two people who live and work in Dubai in my profession, and heard both the good and the hard parts?
  • 8. Do the people who depend on my decision (partner, children, parents) know the plan and agree with it?
  • 9. Do I have a written plan B with a time limit and the money for a return?
  • 10. Will I be fine with the first three months being harder than I expect, and is there someone I can call when they are?

FAQ

Is 8,000 AED a month enough to live in Dubai?

It depends on housing. For a single person who rents a room in a shared flat, cooks at home and uses the metro, 8,000 AED can cover living costs with something left to save; for a person who wants a studio in a central area, eats out often and drives, it will be tight. Build your own budget from current prices on Bayut, Dubizzle, Carrefour online and RTA, and compare it with the salary range for your role in our Salary Index. All figures are orientation, autumn 2026.

Should I move to Dubai without a job offer?

For most specialists the safer order is offer first, then the flight. The employer who hires you arranges and pays for the work permit and visa. If you want to look around first, come as a visitor or on a job seeker visa, with a return date and the money for the trip, and check the current rules on the official portals (ICP, GDRFA). Never pay anyone for a job or a visa.
