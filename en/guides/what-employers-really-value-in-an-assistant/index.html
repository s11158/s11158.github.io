What employers really value in an assistant the owner's view: six qualities, how to show each in a CV, an interview and the first month, and the mistakes that end trust

Candidates prepare for assistant interviews by polishing their list of tools and languages. Owners and executives read that list in ten seconds and then look for something else entirely: can I stop thinking about this part of my life once this person is in the chair. This article from our School of assistants series describes what employers actually value, in their own order of importance: reliability, follow-through, discretion, calm under pressure, proactive communication and written clarity. For each quality we show what it looks like from the owner's chair, how to demonstrate it in a CV, in an interview and in the first month, and which mistakes end trust fastest. Employers can use the same list to decide what to test before an offer.

The owner's view: what an assistant is bought for

Ask a Dubai owner why they want an assistant and the answer is rarely "to book flights". It is time, calm and trust: time returned to the work only they can do, calm because someone else watches the details, trust because that someone sees everything and keeps it inside. Everything on this page follows from those three.

  • Time: every task an assistant takes and finishes without coming back is an hour the owner did not spend; a task that comes back half done costs more than it saved
  • Calm: the owner wants to stop carrying the list in their head; that happens only when nothing has been dropped for long enough that they believe it
  • Trust: the assistant sees money, health, family, deals and conflicts; one leak, even a small one, ends the relationship whatever the skills
  • Tools, languages and experience are the entry ticket, not the decision; they get you the interview, the six qualities below get you the offer and the second year
  • The pay follows the same logic: the market estimate for an executive assistant is 12,000 - 20,000 AED per month and 20,000 - 30,000 with a C-level executive or a private family office; the upper ends are paid for trust and judgement, not for faster typing

Reliability and follow-through

The first quality and the one most often faked in interviews. Reliability means the owner can give you something and forget it. Follow-through means it is not finished until the loop is closed: done, confirmed, the owner told.

  • From the owner's chair: "I asked once, it happened, I was told, and I did not have to ask again"
  • In the CV: years with one named principal (title, not name), a line such as "ran the director's calendar for three years with no missed commitment", references from direct principals available
  • In the interview: concrete stories with a beginning and an end: the problem, what you did, how you confirmed and how you informed; avoid stories that end with "and then it was fine" without saying how you know
  • In the interview, when given a practical task: finish it, say what you would do next and what you would tell the principal; a half-finished task with a good explanation loses to a finished one with a short note
  • In the first month: keep one list where everything lands, close loops the same day, send a short end-of-day summary; by the fourth week the owner should have stopped checking
  • A simple test owners use: did the candidate reply to the interview invitation within a few hours, confirm the day before and arrive early; small reliability predicts large reliability

Discretion and calm under pressure

Two qualities that cannot be learned from a course and that owners weigh more heavily than any skill. Discretion is what you do with what you see. Calm is what you do when the day breaks.

  • From the owner's chair: "I never heard my affairs from anyone else, and when things went wrong this person got quieter, not louder"
  • In the CV: no names of principals or their companies where the role was private, no details that a reader could trace; the restraint itself is the signal, plus a line that references on discretion are available
  • In the interview: when asked about previous principals, talk about your work, not about them; decline politely to share anything that is theirs; an interviewer who hears gossip about a former employer hears what they will become
  • In the interview, for calm: a real story of a day that broke, told without drama, with the sequence of what you did first, second, third and what you changed afterwards
  • In the first month: use the employer's accounts and devices for work, keep nothing on personal devices, say nothing about the principal's affairs to colleagues even in general terms, and ask once, early, which information is private by default
  • Under pressure: slow down for ten seconds before answering, write the next three steps, tell the principal what is happening in one line; owners remember the assistant who stayed level on the worst day of the year

Proactive communication and written clarity

The two qualities that separate an assistant from a messenger. Proactive means the owner learns about a problem from you before it reaches them, with a proposed solution. Written clarity means your message is read once and understood.

  • From the owner's chair: "I am never surprised, and I never have to read anything twice"
  • In the CV: show written English on the CV itself: short, specific, no filler; a CV with vague duties signals vague messages; our Dubai CV format guide and the free CV builder help you structure it
  • In the interview: ask two or three precise questions about the real week, the emergency rules and the principal's preferences; good questions show that you will communicate before acting, not after
  • In the interview, if given a writing task: first sentence says what is needed and by when, then the options, then your recommendation; under 80 words unless the subject needs more
  • In the first month: a daily three-line summary (done, waiting for you, tomorrow), a weekly one-page review, and the habit of flagging a risk the day you see it with one proposed fix
  • Proactive does not mean noisy: owners value the assistant who solved it and told them in one line more than the one who asked five questions and solved nothing; see our article on the first 90 days for how to build the habit

How to prepare: a short plan before the interview

Put the six qualities into evidence before you meet the employer. Owners decide quickly, and the preparation shows.

  • Write six stories, one per quality, each under a minute when spoken: the situation, what you did, how you confirmed and told, what changed afterwards
  • Prepare two referees who can speak to reliability and discretion and tell them to expect a call
  • Rewrite the CV so that every line is a result or a scope, not a duty; remove every adjective about yourself and let the facts carry it
  • Practise a 15-minute inbox triage and a 60-word message on someone else's behalf; our skills checklist shows how to drill each block
  • Prepare three questions about the role that only a serious candidate asks: the real weekly rhythm, the emergency list, what the last assistant did well and where it did not work
  • Arrive early, confirm the day before, follow up with a two-line thank-you the same day; the owner is already testing reliability

The mistakes that end trust

Most assistants are not dismissed for lack of skill. They lose the role because trust broke, usually over something that looked small.

  • A task that was said to be done and was not; one instance is forgiven, a second is remembered forever
  • Sharing a detail about the principal with someone outside, even a harmless one; the owner cannot know where the line will be next time
  • Moving money or signing on the principal's behalf without a written rule that allows it
  • Hiding a mistake instead of reporting it with a fix; owners forgive errors and do not forgive surprises
  • Speaking about a former employer in an interview or at work; it tells the new one what to expect
  • Taking a verbal "handle it" as permission to decide things the principal would have wanted to decide; when unsure, one short question
  • Letting standards drop once trust is earned: the late reply, the unconfirmed booking, the summary that stopped coming; trust is renewed every week, not granted once

FAQ

What do employers look for in an executive assistant?

In practice six things, in this order: reliability (ask once and forget it), follow-through (the loop is closed and the owner told), discretion, calm under pressure, proactive communication and written clarity. Tools, languages and years of experience are the entry ticket that gets the interview; the six qualities get the offer and the second year. Owners test them with concrete stories, a practical task and reference calls with direct principals.

How do I show reliability and discretion in an assistant interview?

Reliability: tell stories with a beginning and an end, including how you confirmed and informed; finish any practical task and say what you would tell the principal; reply to the invitation fast, confirm the day before, arrive early. Discretion: talk about your work, never about former principals or their affairs, decline politely if asked for their details, and offer references on discretion from direct principals.
