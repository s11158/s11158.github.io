Healthcare, transport and daily life in Dubai for a newcomer how the everyday things work in the first months, calmly and without surprises

Most of the anxiety before a move is not about the job. It is about the small things: what happens if I get ill, how do I get to work, is a car necessary, how hot is it really, where do I buy normal food, will anyone understand my English, is there anyone who speaks Russian. The answers are reassuring, and this guide gives them as they are: how the health insurance your employer provides works in practice, where the clinics and pharmacies are, what to do in an emergency, how to choose between the Metro, taxis and a car and what each costs as an orientation, how the year's weather shapes life, which apps run the daily routine, and what the language reality is for a Russian-speaking newcomer. Everything is available in Dubai; the skill is knowing where to look.

How your employer's health insurance works

Health insurance for employees is mandatory in Dubai and the employer arranges and pays for it; it is part of practically every offer. What varies is the plan level, and that decides which clinics you can walk into and what you pay at the desk.

  • You receive an insurance card (physical or in an app) after your residence visa is issued; until then many employers provide temporary cover or reimburse, so ask what applies in your first weeks
  • Every plan has a network: a list of clinics, hospitals and pharmacies where the card works directly. Check the network before choosing a clinic; outside it you pay in full and may claim back only partially or not at all
  • Co-payment: a fixed amount or a percentage you pay per visit or per prescription, typically small on better plans and noticeable on basic ones; it is printed on the card or in the app
  • Pre-approval: some tests, procedures and specialist visits require the clinic to get the insurer's approval first; the clinic does this at the desk, it can take from minutes to a day
  • Dental, optical and maternity are often limited or excluded on basic plans; read these lines, especially if a pregnancy is planned (maternity usually has a waiting period)
  • Chronic medication: declare existing conditions honestly when the policy is set up; most plans cover them after the waiting period, and hiding them can void a claim
  • If a dependant joins you, their cover is a separate policy; see our guide on moving with a family

Clinics, pharmacies and what to do in an emergency

Dubai's healthcare is dense and modern: a clinic within walking distance in almost every residential district, hospitals with emergency departments across the city, pharmacies on most corners, many of them open late or around the clock. Clinics are licensed by the DHA, and licensed doctors are listed in its public register, which you can check.

  • Day-to-day: a general practitioner at a network clinic for everyday illnesses, referrals to specialists through the same clinic; many clinics employ Russian-speaking doctors and receptionists, especially in areas where Russian-speaking families live, and clinic websites usually list the languages a doctor speaks
  • Pharmacies: chains such as Life, Aster and BinSina are everywhere; prescription medicines need a prescription from a UAE-licensed doctor, many common medicines are available over the counter, and prices are regulated. Bring a supply of any regular medication for the first weeks plus the prescription translated into English
  • Emergency: 998 for an ambulance, 999 for police, 997 for fire; emergency departments treat everyone, insurance is sorted afterwards; keep your Emirates ID and insurance card on your phone
  • Check that a medicine you bring is permitted in the UAE: some common medicines abroad are controlled here; the official list and the procedure for bringing prescription medicines are published by the health authorities, check before you fly
  • Mental health, dentistry, physiotherapy and aesthetic medicine are all available at every price point; private pricing varies a lot between clinics, so ask for the price before the appointment if it is outside insurance
  • Our guides for doctors and nurses describe the licensing side (DHA, DataFlow) if you are a medical professional considering the move yourself

The year's weather and how life follows it

The climate is the thing people fear most and adjust to fastest. Dubai has two seasons, and the city is built around them: everything indoors is cooled, and life moves outdoors the moment the heat breaks.

  • October to April: pleasant to warm, 20 to 30 degrees in the day, cool evenings in December and January; this is beach, terrace, running track, outdoor market and festival season, and the time when the city feels at its best
  • May to September: hot and humid, often above 40 degrees in the afternoon; life runs early in the morning and after sunset, indoors otherwise; offices, malls, the Metro, taxis and homes are cooled, and most people barely notice the heat on a working day
  • Summer practicalities: plan outdoor errands before 10 am or after 7 pm, carry water, choose a home and a workplace with covered parking or a short walk to transport, and expect electricity bills to rise with the cooling
  • Rain is rare and brief; when it comes the city slows for a day, so keep it in mind for travel plans
  • Ramadan changes the rhythm for a month each year: shorter working hours in many organisations, restaurants quieter by day and lively at night, and a request to be discreet about eating and drinking in public during daylight; dates move each year, check the calendar
  • Public holidays follow the UAE calendar: national holidays are fixed, religious ones follow the lunar calendar; the employer's HR will know the dates

Daily services: the apps and offices that run your routine

Dubai is one of the most digitised cities to live in. Most of the administrative routine happens in apps, and most of the daily routine can be delivered to the door.

  • Government: UAE Pass is the digital identity you will use for most services; the Emirates ID card is your main document for banks, clinics and contracts; the ICP and GDRFA apps handle visa matters, the RTA app handles Nol, parking, fines and licences; the DEWA app handles electricity and water
  • Banking: open an account once the residence visa is issued (some banks open a basic account on the entry permit); your salary is paid through the Wage Protection System to a UAE account; cards work everywhere, cash is rarely needed
  • Connectivity: a SIM card from du or e& (Etisalat) on arrival, with the residence visa for a full postpaid plan; home internet from the same providers, usually installed within days
  • Food and groceries: Carrefour, Lulu, Union Coop and Spinneys deliver through their apps; Instashop, Talabat, Careem and Noon cover groceries, restaurants and almost everything else; Russian-speaking grocers and bakeries exist in several districts
  • Home: laundry and dry cleaning are collected and delivered; maintenance comes through the building's management; most communities have a gym, a pool and a small supermarket in the building or within walking distance
  • Paperwork rhythm: Emirates ID and residence visa renewal with the employer, tenancy renewal with Ejari, car registration yearly; set reminders for each and keep scans of every document in a cloud folder

Language and the Russian-speaking community

English is the working language of Dubai: shops, clinics, banks, taxis, government services and most workplaces run in it. Arabic is the official language and a plus in some roles, but daily life does not require it. Russian is widely heard and the community is large enough to have its own infrastructure.

  • Your English does not need to be perfect: Dubai is a city of non-native speakers, and clear, simple English is understood and respected everywhere; the level employers ask for depends on the role, see our career guide for Russian-speaking specialists
  • Russian-speaking infrastructure: schools and nursery groups, clinics with Russian-speaking doctors, hairdressers and beauty salons, grocers, lawyers and accountants, churches, sports clubs, community chats for every district and every profession
  • Russian-speaking employers and clients are one reason Dubai employers ask for Russian-speaking staff in beauty, medicine, hospitality, sales and real estate; the request is always for the language and the service level, never for a nationality
  • The practical tip from everyone who settled: spend the first months in English-speaking environments on purpose, even when a Russian-speaking option is easier; it pays back in the second contract
  • Public behaviour is governed by local rules on dress in some places, alcohol (licensed venues and shops for residents), photography of people and public displays of affection; they are reasonable and clearly explained, and respecting them is part of the responsibility you accept when you move
  • Safety is one of the strongest arguments for the move: lost items are returned, children travel alone, late evenings are calm; it is a point every family mentions first

FAQ

Do I need a car in Dubai?

Not necessarily. If home and work are both near a Metro station or a short bus ride apart, the Metro and a Nol card cover most days, with taxis for evenings and the summer. If you live in a community without Metro or have a family, a small rental car (orientation, autumn 2026: roughly 1,500 - 2,500 AED per month plus fuel, Salik tolls and parking) is the usual answer in the first year, with no purchase needed. Check current fares on the RTA site and rental prices directly.

Is healthcare free for employees in Dubai?

Employees are covered by health insurance that the employer must arrange and pay for in Dubai. It is not free healthcare in the public sense: you use the clinics in your plan's network, pay a small co-payment where the plan has one, and some tests and procedures need the insurer's pre-approval, which the clinic obtains. Basic plans limit dental, optical and maternity. Dependants need their own policy, which is the sponsor's responsibility unless the employer includes family cover.
